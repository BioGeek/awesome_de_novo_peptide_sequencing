Nearly every deep-learning de novo sequencing paper reports the same few numbers: peptide recall, amino-acid precision and, more and more often, a curve of precision against coverage. The names are the same from paper to paper; this page checks whether the definitions are, twice over: against what 116 papers say they computed, and against the evaluation code of 45 implementations, version by version.

How it was done. The papers are the catalog’s 124 deep-learning method papers; 8 of them are left out, because their full text is not in the catalog’s PDF library or they were added after it was last read. Five things to know before putting two numbers side by side.

  1. “Peptide precision” is usually a recall. Scored against the search labels, precision is TP / (TP + FP): the predictions that match their label over the predictions made. Recall is TP / (TP + FN): the matches over all labelled spectra, a spectrum the model got wrong or left blank being a false negative. Casanovo’s code calls its peptide figure pep_precision but divides by every labelled spectrum, which is the recall; NovoBench, denovo_benchmarks and PowerNovo2 do the same. The two agree only when the model predicts every spectrum (why). 12 papers define a “peptide precision” over all spectra; 3 define a “peptide recall” over predicted peptides only.
  2. Amino-acid precision and recall are swapped in a whole family of models. Casanovo v3.0 to v3.5 passed the prediction where the label belonged, and v4.0 fixed it. The models that copied the v3 code did not: 9 entries in the table below still swap the two, among them ContraNovo, PrimeNovo, RefineNovo, RankNovo and π-HelixNovo. denovo_benchmarks swapped them from April 2024 to September 2026.
  3. The 0.1 Da mass rule counts some different residues as matches. Under the matching rule DeepNovo introduced, which 44 of the papers state, K predicted for Q (0.036 Da apart), F for oxidised M (0.033 Da) and D for deamidated N (0.000 Da) count as matches, and so does a peptide containing them. ProteoBench’s ppm tolerances and Casanovo-TMT’s exact-peptidoform rule do not count them.
  4. “Peptide AUC” names at least three different areas. NovoBench integrates precision against recall, which can never exceed the peptide recall; others integrate against coverage. For the same 1,000 predictions, half of them right and ranked first, the two give 0.50 and 0.85.
  5. The “truth” is a search engine’s answer. The labels are database-search identifications accepted at a false discovery rate, typically 1%, so some of them are wrong, and only spectra a search could identify are labelled at all. Every figure here measures agreement with the search, not correctness.

The mass-matching rule

The rule was introduced with DeepNovo (2017). A predicted residue counts as a match when its mass is within 0.1 Da of the label’s residue and the prefix mass up to and including it is within 0.5 Da of the label’s prefix. Casanovo added a second pass from the C-terminus, so a residue can also be matched on its suffix mass. A peptide counts as a match when it has as many residues as the label and every one is matched.

From those matches come the usual figures. Amino-acid precision divides the matched residues by the predicted residues, amino-acid recall by the label’s residues. Sorting the predictions by the model’s confidence and computing precision at each depth gives precision against coverage.

Precision, recall, and the spectra left blank

At peptide level, precision is TP / (TP + FP) and recall is TP / (TP + FN). In practice the evaluation code scores each labelled spectrum against its label, and counts it one of three ways:

  • the model predicts the label’s peptide: counted as a true positive;
  • it predicts a different peptide: counted as a false positive, because it asserted something other than the label, and also as a false negative, because the label’s peptide was not found;
  • it predicts nothing, because it abstained, no candidate fitted the precursor mass, or the prediction fell below a confidence threshold: counted as a false negative only.

So peptide precision = matches / predictions made, and peptide recall = matches / all labelled spectra. They differ only by the blanks. Take 100 labelled spectra on which a model predicts 80 and matches the label on 60: precision is 0.75, recall is 0.60. If it predicts all 100, both are the share that matches.

“Counted as” is doing work there, because the label is a database-search identification, not the peptide that produced the spectrum (more below). Two things the counting cannot see:

  • A wrong label turns the counts around. If the search mislabelled a spectrum and the model agrees with it, a false positive is counted as a true positive. If the model gets the real peptide, a true positive is counted as a false positive and a false negative. At a 1% FDR about one label in a hundred is expected to be wrong, and nothing in the evaluation says which.
  • Spectra the search did not identify are not counted at all. The sample holds peptides that produced spectra but that the search missed: not in its database, carrying a modification it did not allow, or scoring below its threshold. They are in neither denominator. A model that sequences them correctly gains nothing, and a model that gets them wrong loses nothing. So “recall” is recall over what the search found, not over the peptides in the sample, and “precision” says nothing about the model’s calls on the spectra de novo sequencing is most often used for.

Casanovo’s evaluate.py returns a figure named pep_precision computed as correct / all spectra passed to it. Since v4.0 every labelled spectrum is passed, blanks included, so for the example above it prints 0.60: the recall. Several papers call it “precision at coverage 1” (Pairwise, Modanovo), which is accurate only when the model has predicted every spectrum; once it leaves some blank, it is the recall. NovoBench, denovo_benchmarks and PowerNovo2 compute the same quantity under the name “peptide precision”, and so do the papers that use their code. The reverse also happens: a few papers divide by the predictions made, which is a precision, and call it recall.

Two things follow from matching on mass. I and L weigh the same, so they always match, with no I/L rule needed. And so do pairs that differ by less than 0.1 Da: under the rule, PEPTIDEQ counts as a match for PEPTIDEK (1 means yes). PepPr points out that this makes deamidated N equal to D and scores its deamidation calls on identity instead; Casanovo-TMT rejects the rule for the same reason and requires the exact peptidoform. ProteoBench keeps the rule’s shape but in ppm: 20 ppm per residue and 50 ppm on the prefix. K and Q are 284 ppm apart, so there they count as different.

The truth is a search engine’s answer

Every one of these figures compares a prediction with a label, and the label is not the peptide that produced the spectrum. It is the peptide a database search assigned to it, kept if it passed a false discovery rate threshold, typically 1%. That has three consequences for what “precision” and “recall” mean here.

  • Some labels are wrong. At 1% FDR about one accepted identification in a hundred is expected to be false, and the FDR is itself an estimate from decoys, not a count. Where a de novo call disagrees with a wrong label, the call is scored as an error although it may be the right one. A perfect sequencer would not score 1, and two models that differ by less than the label error rate cannot be told apart on that evidence alone.
  • Only the identifiable spectra are labelled. A spectrum enters the test set only if a search engine found a confident match in its database. That leaves out most of what de novo sequencing is for: peptides absent from the database, unexpected modifications, poor or chimeric spectra. Recall over labelled spectra says how well a model reproduces the search on the spectra the search could already do, not how it fares on the rest.
  • The labels carry the search’s own choices. Which engine, which database, which modifications were allowed and which threshold was applied all differ between benchmarks, and change between versions of the same benchmark: the nine-species versions relabel the same spectra differently. A search engine also cannot tell I from L, so neither can its labels.

So the field’s “precision” and “recall” are agreement rates with a reference that is itself uncertain. Precision in the strict sense, the share of predictions that are right, cannot be measured on the unlabelled spectra at all, which is why estimates of the error rate that need no search engine are now appearing (below).

What the papers say about it

44 papers state the 0.1 / 0.5 Da rule. 17 count a residue or a peptide correct only on an exact sequence match, and 4 use a stricter mass rule or compare position by position: SMSNet wants the residue mass exact to 0.0001 Da and the prefix within 0.03 Da, and PepNet and DPST compare position by position, so one inserted residue makes everything after it wrong. 51 papers state no rule at all. On I and L, 24 papers say they count as equal and 89 do not say; that matters only for the papers that compare strings.

The wording varies more than the rule. Most papers write that the prefix or the suffix mass must agree; RankNovo’s text says both must. Several measure a peptide as a string while measuring residues by mass, so their peptide and amino-acid numbers rest on different definitions. And the names do not follow the denominators: the table marks the 12 papers that call a recall “precision” and the 3 that call a precision “recall”.

Forward only, or forward and back

DeepNovo’s loop walks both peptides from the N-terminus. When the prefix masses drift apart by more than 0.5 Da, as they do after an inserted or wrong-mass residue, nothing after the drift can match again. Casanovo’s second pass from the C-terminus recovers the residues after the error. Truth PEPTIDEK against the prediction PEPGTIDEK matches 3 residues forward only and 8 with Casanovo’s rule. 12 implementations in the table are forward only: the DeepNovo family, GraphNovo, DIANovo, SMSNet, DiNovo and InstaNovo, whose matcher carries an “adapted from Casanovo” comment but is DeepNovo’s loop. Their amino-acid numbers come out lower on the same predictions; their peptide numbers do not change, because a correct peptide never drifts.

Precision and recall, swapped

Casanovo’s aa_match_batch returns the residue counts in the order its two peptide arguments came in, and aa_match_metrics expects the true count first. From v3.0.0 to v3.5.0 the validation step passed the prediction first, so the number printed as amino-acid precision was the recall and the other way round; commit 3a18fed fixed it for v4.0.0. For truth PEPTIDEK and prediction PEPTIDE, precision is 1.000 and recall 0.875; the swapped code prints 0.875 as precision. Peptide figures are unaffected, because the match itself is symmetric.

Most of the models built on Casanovo v3 kept the swap, and their latest code still has it: ContraNovo, PrimeNovo and every copy of it (π-PrimeNovo, ScopeNovo), RefineNovo, RankNovo, DiffNovo, Transformer-DIA and DiffNovo-DIA, and every tag of π-HelixNovo. TSARseqNovo copied the fixed v4.0 file and kept the old argument order; pUniFind’s own copy swaps them too. XuanjiNovo swapped them at its v1.1 tag and has since fixed it, and π-MNovo fixed it in v0.2.0. The multi-tool benchmark denovo_benchmarks reversed the arguments in April 2024 and fixed it on 26 September 2026 (commit 3a92eba), so its “AA precision” from that period is amino-acid recall.

Which spectra are counted

Up to v3.5.0, Casanovo dropped every spectrum whose prediction was empty or malformed from both sides of the comparison, so its peptide figure was computed over the spectra that got a usable prediction, and a model that abstained more scored higher. ContraNovo, the PrimeNovo family, RefineNovo, DiffNovo and π-HelixNovo inherited that. From v4.0 Casanovo keeps them; NovoBench, AdaNovo and dIon keep them too.

InstaNovo excludes empty predictions from precision only, which is what precision means. But what counts as empty changed: before 1.2.0, beam search returned nothing when no beam fitted the precursor mass, so precision was computed over precursor-matched predictions only. From 1.2.0 those beams are kept, ranked after the matching ones, so InstaNovo precision before and after 1.2.0 is not like-for-like; the recall is. denovo_benchmarks made the opposite move on 8 February 2025: until then its “Pep precision” was correct over predicted spectra, a precision, and from then on over all labelled spectra, a recall.

One tool, several versions

Casanovo.

  • v1.1 to v2.1 matched on the mass before each residue in a single pass.
  • v3.0 rewrote that as the prefix-then-suffix rule used since.
  • v3.0 to v3.5 swapped precision and recall and dropped empty predictions. v4.0 fixed both, but in validation a spectrum whose beams were all discarded silently shifts the rest of its batch onto the wrong truth.
  • v5.0 to v5.1.2: casanovo sequence --evaluate counted the padding of the true sequence as residues, so it printed a peptide precision of 0. v5.2.0 fixed it.
  • v5: the validation code still splits a peptide before every capital letter, so M[Oxidation] becomes two pieces of mass 0, and any modified residue matches any other.
  • The confidence that orders a precision-coverage curve changed three times: the mean of the residue scores minus 1 off-precursor (v3 to v4.3), the product minus 1 (v5.0 to v5.1), and the product with no penalty (v5.2 on). The same model gives three different curves.

InstaNovo.

  • 0.1.0 to 0.1.7 had the resynchronisation step inverted: after the first mismatch it advanced the peptide that was already ahead, so no later residue could match. GGPEPTIDE against NPEPTIDE matches 0 residues in 0.1.x and 7 from 1.0.0. Peptide figures were unaffected. InstaNovo-P still ships the 0.1 step, including in its PTM matcher.
  • 1.0.0 made its amino-acid error rate count I and L as equal; 0.1 counted every I/L call as an error.
  • 1.2.0 changed which predictions are empty, as above.

denovo_benchmarks has had four evaluators: correct but over predicted spectra only (March 2024), swapped (from April 2024), swapped and over all labelled spectra (from February 2025), and correct over all labelled spectra (from September 2026). ProteoBench stored no AUC until 4 August 2026, and has since integrated precision against coverage at every distinct score.

Smaller differences that still change a number

  • DeepNovo’s peptide rule ignores the prediction’s length: a peptide counts as a match when every residue of the label is matched, so PEPTIDEK counts as a match for PEPTIDE. Its DIA and PointNovo descendants, given more than one candidate per spectrum, keep the candidate closest to the label; with the default of one it makes no difference.
  • DiffNovo logs, per batch, the better of its two decoders, which no deployed system could choose, and its peptide slot holds an amino-acid value.
  • RankNovo averages per-batch peptide recalls without weighting them by batch size, and weights the amino-acid figure by the first candidate’s length.
  • MemNovo strips every modification before matching and its mass table has no glycine, so a deleted G costs nothing; under its PrimeNovo model name it falls back to counting letters regardless of order.
  • PowerNovo2’s precision-recall plot counts each correct residue as a negative as well, which caps the plotted precision at 0.5, and a results file holding any empty prediction is silently skipped.
  • PTM precision and recall in NovoBench and AdaNovo inherit the mass rule, so F in place of an oxidised M counts toward PTM recall.
  • Unknown tokens weigh 0 in Casanovo and its copies, so a label with an unparsed modification is scored with every later prefix mass off; InstaNovo and the DeepNovo family refuse it.

Three areas called AUC

  • NovoBench (and LIPNovo and SemiNovo, which copy it) integrates precision against recall: the area can never exceed the peptide recall, and wrong predictions add no width.
  • Precision against coverage, coverage being the share of all spectra predicted, is what denovo_benchmarks (at 200 downsampled points), ProteoBench (at every distinct score), dIon and Casanovo’s unreleased dev branch integrate. The Pairwise scripts compute coverage over predicted spectra only.
  • InstaNovo integrates peptide precision against recall at 21 confidence quantiles.

On the same predictions the first two give 0.50 and 0.85; neither is wrong, but they are not the same number.

Metrics beyond recall

RankNovo’s PMD and RMD

RankNovo reranks the candidates of several sequencers, and to train the reranker it introduced two measures of how far a candidate is from the label. Both are training targets, not evaluation metrics: the paper still reports peptide recall and amino-acid precision, and the released code computes those with the swapped Casanovo v3 call.

PMD, Peptide Mass Deviation, is an edit distance in which inserting or deleting a residue costs one unit and substituting one costs the mass difference; the released code fixes the unit at 51.4238 Da. It is not the difference between the two peptides’ masses: GG against N, which weigh the same, costs 2.11 units, a missing residue costs 1 unit whatever it weighs, and K for Q costs 0.0007.

RMD, Residual Mass Deviation, gives each residue of the candidate the signed offset between its prefix mass and the nearest prefix mass of the label. For PEPGGK against PEPNK it is -57.02 Da at the first G and 0.00 Da at the end, where the masses agree again. In the released inference code only the PMD head chooses the candidate; the RMD head is computed and discarded. No other repository implements either.

Error rates without a search engine

A recall needs labels, which only database search provides, so the field has started to estimate error rates on unlabelled spectra: Winnow turns calibrated confidence into an FDR (the mean of one minus the confidence above a threshold); DiNovo maps predictions to a target-decoy protein database; π-NovoQC compares counterpart spectra; Casanovo’s 2023 preprint and Casanovo-TMT count matches to a shuffled or decoy digest, the latter saying plainly that this is not a search-engine FDR; and Cascadia counts a DIA prediction correct when it matches the reference proteome exactly.

Other measures

Casanovo 5 reports a calibration curve. DiffuNovo reports the error of the predicted peptide’s mass and the share within 0.001 Da. BiATNovo adds a position-weighted BLEU and a mass-aware alignment score, the QuasiNovo thesis a longest common subsequence anchored on terminal masses, OmniNovo backbone and modification-site accuracy, Spectralis precision and recall of its fragment-bin corrections, and NovoCRF recall split by how much fragment-ion evidence each spectrum holds.

Reading a number

Before comparing two published figures, find out:

  1. Which code, at which version, computed it. A Casanovo v3 descendant and a Casanovo v4 one print the same labels for different quantities.
  2. The denominator. All labelled spectra, spectra with a prediction, or spectra with a well-formed prediction.
  3. The residue rule. The 0.1 / 0.5 Da mass rule, ppm, or an exact sequence.
  4. For a curve, which confidence orders it, and what the area is taken against.
